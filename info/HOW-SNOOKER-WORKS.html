This page explains how Game 6: Snooker works inside. It covers why the balls are 1.8 times real size, how a hit runs through the pack of reds, how the referee knows which ball you were on and what a foul is worth, and how Marker, the computer, picks his shot. Claude Code (an AI coding agent) wrote the whole game from a plan, starting from a copy of Billiards. Players don't need any AI to play. The demos on this page use the same numbers and rules as the game, so you can try them yourself.

1The table, and why the balls are 1.8 times real size

Snooker uses the same table as Billiards: 3.57 m × 1.78 m inside the cushions. The game works in metres, so every distance is real. The yellow, brown and green sit on the baulk line (yellow on the right of the D as you look up the table), the blue on the centre spot, the pink halfway between the centre and the top cushion, and the black 32.4 cm from the top cushion.

Billiards plays its three balls at twice real size, so they show up on a phone. Snooker can't: the pack of 15 reds sits between the pink and the black, and the gap is only 56.8 cm. From the pink to the far side of the black takes about 10.9 ball radii, so the balls can be at most about 1.98 times real size. At 2x the back row of reds would overlap the black. The game started at 1.6x; after a test on a phone found the balls too small, they were made 1.8x, which still leaves 5 cm behind the pack. Try it:

Try it: the pack between the pink and the black, at any ball size

The numbers (from snooker.html) 
L = 3.569, W = 1.778            // the playing area, metres
SIZE = 1.8                      // balls against real size
R = 0.02625 * SIZE              // ball radius: 4.7 cm
POCKET = 1.6                    // pockets against real size
pink  = L * 0.75                // halfway to the top cushion
black = L - 0.324
RACK_GAP = 0.0001               // 0.1 mm between racked reds

The pockets are 1.6 times real size. Bigger balls in the same pockets make potting harder, and bigger pockets make it easier, so the two were tested together. In a test of careful pots (random positions, a small wobble on the aim), 1.8x balls with 1.6x pockets go in 55% of the time: the same as in Billiards (55%), and as with the first 1.6x balls (54%). With 1.8x pockets it would be 63%. The physics (sliding, rolling, spin, cushions, pocket jaws) is the one from Billiards: see How Billiards works.

2How a hit runs through the pack

Billiards never has more than three balls on the table, and they hardly ever touch. A snooker pack is fifteen balls packed together, so the break-off sends a hit through a chain of touching balls in one instant. The game racks the reds a tenth of a millimetre apart, so every contact in the chain has its own exact time. It then works them out one at a time, in a fixed order, which is why the same break-off always spreads the pack the same way.

Try it: the white hits a straight line of touching reds full on

Ball meets ball (from snooker.html) 
// vrel: how fast the two close along the line of centres
j = (1 + E_BALL) / 2 * vrel;     // E_BALL = 0.95
j = max(j, (vrel + V_SEP) / 2);  // V_SEP = 0.002 m/s
b1.v -= j;  b2.v += j;

Balls keep 95% of their energy, so each one in the chain keeps a little speed and the push mostly passes on, like a Newton's cradle. V_SEP came from testing. Balls lying against each other in a cluster, pushed together by their spin, were bumping thousands of times at under 1 mm/s (one frame of a test worked out 1,157 hits). Now two balls that meet that slowly part at 2 mm/s, which you could never see. A full-power shot into the pack makes about 50 hits instead of 150. At most 60 hits are worked out in one 1/600 s step, so a frame never takes long.

3The ball on, and what a foul is worth

At every shot you are on something: a red, or after potting a red any colour, or, once the reds are gone, one colour (yellow, green, brown, blue, pink, then black). Real snooker players name the colour they are going for; in this game you don't have to: the first colour your white hits is the one you chose. While the balls run, the game writes down what happens: which ball the white hit first, which balls dropped. Then a referee function reads that list and decides.

4Free ball, touching ball and the respotted black
After a foul

The other player chooses: play on from where the balls are, or make the fouler play again. A box asks you; Marker decides for himself (he plays on when he has a fair pot).

Free ball: if the foul leaves you unable to hit both edges of any ball on in a straight line, any ball counts as the ball on, and scores as the ball on. A line-of-sight helper works this out: it tries nine straight lines from one edge of each ball on to the other.

Touching ball

When the white comes to rest against a ball (within 2 mm), the game says so and you play away from it. If that ball is on, it counts as already hit, so playing away is not a miss.

The end of a frame

The first score or foul on the last black ends the frame. If the scores are then level, the black goes back on its spot, a coin toss says who plays at it from the D, and the first score or foul wins.

The game shows the points left and when a player needs snookers (more behind than there is to score). Left out, as agreed in the plan: the "foul and a miss" rule.

5How Marker chooses his shot

Marker isn't an AI that learned snooker. He is a short, plain program that tries shots in the game's own physics, with the game's own referee, before he plays one, so his plans always match what will really happen.

1. Make a listFrom where the white lies (or the best places in the D): a pot of each ball on into each pocket it lines up with, at a few paces and spins, easiest first; thin and thick safety hits on the nearest balls on; and, at the start of a frame, a few break-offs.
→
2. Try each onePlay it in the sim and let the referee score it. Points count (less for a hard pot he might miss), plus how easy his next ball is: that is how he picks his colour after a red. If he doesn't score, a hard shot for you is good, and leaving you snookered is better.
→
3. Look ahead (Hard)For his best two pots, search again from where the balls stop, and add the best follow-up.
→
4. Play it, with a wobbleHis aim and pace are a little off, by a random amount that shrinks with the level.

Each tried shot is a full shot with 22 balls, so to give him time the physics step was made about 1.5 times faster, with exactly the same results (290 shots compared ball by ball). He thinks about 10 ms each frame while his cue drifts across what he is considering, and he chooses within 2 seconds even on a slow phone. In Node, over 30 six-red frames each, Hard beat Easy 30–0 (1,831 points to 288, best break 60) and Medium beat Easy 30–0. In five full 15-red frames Hard made breaks of 30 or more, the best 42.

6The Snooker school, the two views, and how you play
Snooker school
  • Five set-up shots: the long pot, stun for the black, screw back for the pink, the break-off and escaping a snooker; most have a circle the white must stop in.
  • Each was placed by a search over thousands of shots in the sim. Every one can be made. In the stun drill no middle-of-the-ball hit stays in the circle (over that distance a stun needs a touch of screw), and in the screw drill only screw does.
  • A sixth, timed challenge: clear the colours from yellow to black. The frame's own referee judges it, and your best is kept.
Top view and Eye view
  • Top: the table from above. Aim by swinging the cue stick round the white; shoot by pulling the power cue on the right down and letting go. The balls you may hit glow, and the ring turns red if the white would hit the wrong ball first.
  • Eye: low behind the white, in 3D with three.js, as in Billiards; Power slider and SHOOT. Without WebGL it falls back to a hand-drawn eye view.
  • Numbers on balls (N) prints each ball's value on it, for small screens and colour-blind players.
Sound and phones
  • Every sound is made in code: the cue, the clack of the balls, a crack for the break-off, the cushions, a ball dropping, a tock when a colour goes back on its spot, and applause for a break of 50, a century or a clearance.
  • On a narrow phone the portraits beside the table are left out, and on a phone held sideways the game area uses nearly the whole screen. Drawn text never gets smaller than a readable size, and in full screen on a phone held sideways a ball is about 9 px across (22 px on a laptop).
7What Claude Code built, and how it was checked

Claude Code wrote the game from the plan, starting from a copy of Billiards. The only borrowed code is the three.js library for the Eye view. The game file has three scripts, plus three.js as plain text:

sim: the rulesThe table, the 22 balls and their physics, the referee, the Snooker school and Marker. It draws nothing, so test programs can play whole frames without a browser.
→
eye3d: the 3D viewBuilds the table, the balls in their colours, the cue and the hand in three.js, and moves them each frame from the game.
→
game-ui: the screenThe Top view, controls, menus, sound and saving. It asks the rules what happened and shows it.

How it was checked
  • Node tests (95 in all) play shots with the real rules. The pack fits and the same break-off always gives the same result. A line of touching reds passes the hit to the far one. No ball ever overlaps another or leaves the table. Every scoring and foul rule is checked, 40 whole frames all finish with scores that add up, and every Snooker school shot can be made.
  • Computer-v-computer frames in Node checked that each level of Marker is stronger than the one below, and how long he takes to choose.
  • A test browser opened the game at laptop, tablet and phone sizes. It aimed, pulled the power cue, played frames, drills and the clearance in both views, and checked the sounds, the layout and that no control covers the cloth.
