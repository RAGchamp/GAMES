What is this page and how does AI play a part?

This page explains how Game 7: Play with a friend works inside. You play Billiards against a friend, each of you on your own computer or phone, wherever you are. It covers how the two screens talk through an online room, why a shot needs only a few numbers, why the striker's screen has the final say, how you see your friend line up, and what happens when someone's connection drops. Claude Code (an AI coding agent) wrote it from a plan, starting from a copy of Billiards. Players don't need any AI to play. Snooker with a friend is planned too, but is on hold for now, so this page is about Billiards.

1Two screens and a room between them

A web page can't reach another person's computer by itself. So both pages connect to a shared room kept by an online service, Google Firebase (its Realtime Database, on the free plan). Each player writes their moves into the room and reads the other player's moves from it. Firebase tells a page within a fraction of a second when something in the room changes. The games stay plain web pages, served from GitHub Pages, and no server of our own has to be kept running.

The room code is 6 characters from 32 letters and digits, leaving out 0, O, 1 and I so it can't be misread when said out loud. That makes about a billion codes. The link is the game's address with ?room=K7QPZM on the end, so a friend can just tap it in WhatsApp.

One file does all the talking. Every call to Firebase is in online.js, behind a few functions: create a room, join it, send a move, hear about each move, and hear when the friend comes or goes. Moving to another service later would change only that file.

Two players, two colours. The creator always plays the white and the friend the yellow. Inside the rules (the sim script, copied unchanged from Billiards) the players are simply 'white' and 'yellow', so both screens hold exactly the same match and it can be compared directly.

2A game is a list of numbered moves

Everything that changes a game is a move: a small message with a number, who sent it, and what kind it is. Moves are only ever added, never changed, and both pages apply them in order, 1, 2, 3…. If two moves arrive the wrong way round, the page holds the later one until the earlier one comes. If both players try to write move 7 at the same moment, the room's rules let only the first one in; the other page waits and tries again as move 8. Step through a short game:

A page ignores a move that doesn't fit: a shot from the player whose turn it isn't, or a toss from anyone but the creator. Your own page shows your shot as you play it, so when your own move comes back from the room it is skipped (each one carries a short id). A move can be at most 12,000 characters; a whole match fits easily.

3Why a shot is six numbers, and why the striker has the final say

Billiards keeps its physics apart from the drawing code. One function plays a whole shot from the place of the white, the aim, the power and the spin. So the striker sends just those six numbers, and the friend's page plays the same shot with the same code: their cue turns to the aim, strikes, and the balls run on both screens.

1. Striker shootsThe shot move goes to the room as the cue strikes.
→
2. Both play itThe friend's cue swings to the aim in half a second and strikes; the balls run on both screens.
→
3. Striker scores itWhen the balls stop, the striker's page runs the referee (applyShot) and sends the result.
→
4. Friend takes itThe friend's page takes the result as final. Any ball that ended a little off slides into place in a quarter of a second.

Why not let both pages score the shot themselves? Different browsers and phones can work out sin and cos very slightly differently, and every time one ball hits another, a tiny difference in where they meet grows. So the friend's playback is for show, and the striker's result is what counts. Try it on a simplified table:

The shot

The result and the slide (from billiards-friend.html) 
// striker's page, when the balls stop
r = applyShot(m, state, start);
send('result', { m: snapshot(m), r });

// friend's page: the result is final
game.m = d.m;
slide = { t: 0, from: whereMyBallsAre,
          to: m.balls };   // 0.25 s

With only three balls, a shot usually has just a few ball-on-ball hits, so a difference of a billionth of a degree stays far too small to see (try 10⁻⁹ above). A shot with several hits multiplies it each time, and a bigger difference can send the red somewhere else entirely. In the game's own test, 200 shots between two pages gave the same match on both after every shot, and the friend's playback ended exactly where the striker's did (the largest gap was 0 m). Two pages in the same browser always agree to the last digit; the slide is there for when two different browsers don't. The demo's table has no spin and no pockets, so it is a sketch of the effect, not the game's physics.

Chance, too, is decided on one side. The coin toss for who breaks is made on the creator's page and sent as the toss move, and anything else left to chance happens on the striker's page and arrives in the result. So both screens always agree. The striker's page is trusted, which is fine between friends; checking shots for cheating is left out on purpose.

4Watching your friend line up

While the striker lines up, their aim, power, spin and (with the white in hand) the white's place go to the room's live slot. It isn't a move: each update simply overwrites the last one, at most 10 times a second, and only when something changes. The last update always goes, so the friend's cue ends exactly where the striker's stopped. The watching page doesn't jump its cue to each update. Every frame it moves part of the way there, so the cue glides even when updates arrive late or bunched up. Drag on the table to aim (or leave it to swing by itself):

Try it: the striker's cue (white) and what the friend sees (yellow)

The friend's cue

Sending and following (from online.js and billiards-friend.html) 
// striker: at most every 100 ms; the last one always goes
sendLive({ n: shotNumber, a: aim, p: power,
           s: [spinX, spinY], w: whiteInHand });

// friend, every frame (dt in seconds)
k = min(1, dt * 14);
aim   += (live.a - aim) * k;
power += (live.p - power) * k;

Each update carries the shot number, so a late update from the last turn is never shown on the next one. An update can be at most 600 characters (one is about 60). In the game's test, the friend's cue never turned more than 0.06 radians (about 3°) between two frames, and when the striker stopped, both cues pointed the same way to within a ten-thousandth of a radian. The friend also sees the guide line and the power cue being pulled back.

5When a connection drops, and coming back

Each player's entry in the room has a here flag. A page sets it when it connects, and asks Firebase in advance to clear it if the connection is lost. So when a phone loses its signal or a tab is closed, the server clears the flag itself, and the other page hears about it. A small light by each portrait shows it:

Try it: Lee has been away for…

  • Green: here.
  • Amber: dropped out a moment ago. A screen locking or a weak signal does only this. The message along the bottom of the table says your friend seems to have dropped out.
  • Grey: gone. After 30 seconds away, a box says your friend has lost the connection, with Keep waiting (as long as you like) and End the match. There is no automatic win. When they come back, the box closes by itself with "Lee is back".
Reloading and catching up

Because every move is kept in order, a page that reloads, comes back after a lost signal, or is opened on another device reads all the moves from move 1 and rebuilds the same game, straight to the table, without the animations. While catching up, a shot whose result is already in the list is skipped (the result has the balls), and old reactions don't pop up again. The game's test reloads the friend's page after 200 shots and carries on with both pages in step.

Your seat is yours

The room remembers each player by their Google sign-in ID, a hidden code, not a name. Sign in with the same account on another phone or laptop, open the link, and you get your seat back. A third person who opens the link is told the game is full. The title screen offers to go back to a game that is still going.

Phones

In the games against Marker, hiding the page pauses the game. With a friend it doesn't: their shot may arrive while your screen is off, and when you look again your page catches up on the moves. While you are in a game with a friend the page also asks the phone to keep its screen on (the browser's Screen Wake Lock), so it doesn't go dark while you watch them line up.

6Keeping the room safe and private
The database rules
  • Only a signed-in player can make a room, and only with themselves as the creator.
  • Only the two players in a room can write to it. Each can write only their own moves, and an existing move can never be changed.
  • The room's size is checked: a code of 6 allowed letters, a move of at most 12,000 characters, the live slot at most 600.
  • A room can be deleted only once it is more than 24 hours old.
App Check, and the free plan

The page's Firebase settings are public (they have to be, in a web page). On top of the rules, Firebase App Check lets only pages served from the game's GitHub Pages address use the database, so a copy of the page elsewhere is refused.

Firebase never deletes anything by itself, and a scheduled clean-up needs the paid plan. So each time a room is made, the page also deletes up to 10 rooms more than a day old. That keeps everything on the free plan, which allows far more than two friends need.

Your name stays yours

Google is used only to sign in. Your Google name, email and photo are never written to the room or shown. Your friend sees only the portrait you chose and its name. If you both chose the same portrait, your friend's gets a different frame colour and "(friend)" after the name on your screen.

Your results against each friend (played, won, best break) are kept on your own device, under each friend's sign-in ID, separate from your games against Marker.

How it was checked
  • The room (Phase 1 test): two test pages join by code and see the same moves in the same order, each in well under a second. A third player can't join a full room. A closed page shows amber, then grey after the grace time. Reopening the link takes back the seat and catches up on every move. Old rooms are deleted, and wrong or old codes and oversized moves are refused with a clear message.
  • A whole game (Phase 2 test): two pages play 200 shots against each other, with the same match on both after every shot. Then a reload mid-game, giving up, a rematch where the other player breaks, a full game, and the results against the friend. All 15 checks pass.
  • Watching (Phase 3 tests): the friend's cue, power, spin and white in hand follow the striker smoothly. Reactions pop up on both screens, at most one per 1.5 s, and not again after a reload. The "lost the connection" box comes and goes, and hiding a page doesn't pause it.
  • Still to come (Phase 5): the owner and a friend playing from two homes, laptop and phone, on Wi-Fi and mobile data.
