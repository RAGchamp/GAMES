What is this page and how does AI play a part?

This page explains how Game 5: Billiards works inside: how a struck ball slides and then rolls, why screw brings it back, where two balls go when they meet, how side changes the angle off a cushion, how the referee scores a shot, and how Marker, the computer, picks his shot. Claude Code (an AI coding agent) wrote the whole game from a plan, and a person tested every version on a computer and a phone. Players don't need any AI to play. The demos on this page use the same formulas and numbers as the game, so you can try them yourself.

1The table, in real sizes (nearly)

English billiards is played on a full-size snooker table: 3.57 m × 1.78 m inside the cushions, about 12 ft × 6 ft. The game works in metres and seconds, so every distance is real: the baulk line is 73.7 cm from the bottom cushion, the D has a radius of 29.2 cm, and the red's spot is 32.4 cm from the top cushion.

Two sizes changed after testing on a phone
  • The balls are twice real size (10.5 cm across instead of 5.25 cm). Real-size balls on a whole table looked like dots on a phone.
  • The pockets are 1.6 times real size. Doubling them with the balls made potting too easy: bigger balls and pockets on the same table look wider from where the ball is. At 2× a careful pot went in 63% of the time; at 1.6× it is 47%. The corner opening is about 1.8 balls wide, close to a real table.
The numbers (from billiards.html) 
L = 3.569, W = 1.778            // the playing area, metres
R = 0.02625 * SIZE              // ball radius; SIZE = 2
POCKET = 1.6                    // pockets against real size
MU_S = 0.2                      // sliding friction
MU_R = 0.01                     // rolling friction
E_BALL = 0.95, E_CUSH = 0.75    // how bouncy balls and cushions are
VMAX = 7.0                      // the white's speed at full power, m/s
2A ball slides, then rolls: that is how top and screw work

When the cue hits the white in the middle, the white slides across the cloth without turning. Friction then grips its bottom and makes it turn, until it is rolling (turning exactly as fast as it moves). Rolling balls slow down very gently, so they run a long way.

Hit the white high (top) and it starts with forward spin; hit it low (screw) and it spins backwards. When the white hits another ball full on, the push goes almost all into that ball and the white nearly stops, but its spin carries on: forward spin makes it follow, backward spin pulls it back towards you. That is the whole secret of top and screw.

Try it: a straight shot up the table at the red, 0.8 m away

Each tiny step, in code 
// v: speed; r: spin, as the speed it would roll at
slip = v - r;
if (slip != 0) {                 // sliding
  v -= MU_S * g * dir(slip) * dt;
  r += 2.5 * MU_S * g * dir(slip) * dt;
} else {                         // rolling
  v -= MU_R * g * dir(v) * dt;  r = v;
}
// the cue hits h above the middle: spin r = 2.5 v h / R
// ball on ball: speeds swap along the line of centres
// (95% kept); the spin is not touched

The 2.5 comes from the shape of a solid ball. The game runs this 600 times a second for every ball, and finds the exact moment of every hit, so a fast ball never passes through another one.

3Where two balls go: the 90° rule

When the white hits a ball off-centre (a cut), the other ball goes off along the line joining the two centres at the moment they touch. If the white is sliding (a stun), it goes off at right angles to that line. If it is rolling, its forward spin bends it further forward. The game's aim guide shows exactly this: the ring is where the balls touch (the ghost ball) and the yellow line is where the hit ball goes.

Try it: change how thick the hit is

Half-ball and full-ball
  • A full-ball hit (0°) sends the red straight on. A stunned white stops dead; a rolling one follows.
  • A half-ball hit (the white's middle aimed at the red's edge) is a cut of 30°. A rolling white then goes off about 32° from its first path in the game (players learn about 34° on a real table): the angle used for in-offs and cannons.
  • The thinner the cut, the less the red moves and the more the white keeps going.
// the hit: the white keeps its speed across the line of centres
// and 2.5% along it (E_BALL = 0.95); its spin is not touched
after = v - 0.975 * (v · n) * n
// then the cloth turns slide into roll:
rolling = (5 * after + 2 * spin) / 7
4Side: changing the angle off a cushion

Hit the white to one side of its middle and it spins about an upright axis, like a top. That does nothing on the open cloth, but when the white touches a cushion its spinning surface grips the rubber and drags it along. Side the same way the ball is running (running side) widens the angle it comes off at; side the other way (check side) narrows it. Without side, a ball comes off a cushion with 75% of its speed across the cushion and 96% of its speed along it.

Try it: the white comes into the top cushion at 45°

At the cushion, in code 
out_across = 0.75 * in_across        // E_CUSH
grip = clamp(side_spin * 2/7,
             ±0.25 * 1.75 * in_across) // MU_CUSH
out_along = (in_along + grip) * 0.96 // CUSH_KEEP
side_spin -= 2.5 * grip              // side is used up

In the game you choose spin by tapping a spot on a big picture of the white: above the middle for top, below for screw, to one side for side (keys I K J L). The cloth slowly wears side away, so it matters most on the first cushion. The Spin school has five set-up shots, each placed (by a computer search) where only the right spin leaves the white in the target circle.

5How the referee reads a shot

While the balls run, the game writes down everything that happens, in order: which ball hit which, which cushions were touched, which balls dropped into which pocket, and when the white crossed the baulk line. When everything is still, a referee function reads only that list and works out the score. Because it never looks at the picture, every rule can be tested with a list of made-up events.

Scores in one shot add up, up to 10. You keep playing while every shot scores; the run is your break. A potted red goes back on its spot; a potted white or yellow waits off the table until its owner's turn, then is played from the D.

        

      

    

  


  
  

    

      
6How Marker chooses his shot

      
Marker isn't an AI that learned billiards. He is a short, plain program that tries shots in the game's own physics before he plays one, so his plans always match what will really happen.

      

        
1. Make a listFrom where his ball lies (or the best spots in the D): a pot of each ball into each pocket it lines up with, and hits from thick to thin for cannons and in-offs, at a few paces, with spin on Medium and Hard.

        
→

        
2. Try each onePlay it in the sim, ask the referee for the points, and look at where the balls stop: an easy next shot for him is good; on Hard, an easy shot for you is bad.

        
→

        
3. Look ahead (Hard)For his best two scoring shots, search again from where the balls stop, and add the best follow-up.

        
→

        
4. Play it, with a wobbleHis aim and pace are a little off, by a random amount that shrinks with the level.

Marker thinks a few shots each frame, so the screen never freezes while his cue drifts across what he is considering. On a laptop Hard decides in about a third of a second; after 1.5 s of thinking he plays his best shot so far, so a slow phone never keeps you waiting. In 50 computer-v-computer games, Hard scored 6.8 times as many points as Easy and won all 50; Medium beat Easy 50 times out of 51.

        

      

    

  


  
  

    

      
7Two views, and how you play

      

        

          Top view (the default)
          
    
            
  • The table from above, drawn once and kept, so each frame only draws the balls, the cue and the guide: smooth on phones.
    • 
            
  • Aim by pressing on the cue stick and swinging it round the white; fine aim with ◀ ▶.
    • 
            
  • Shoot by pulling the power cue on the right down and letting go: the further you pull, the harder the shot (push it back up to cancel).
    • 
            
  • In a game, your portrait and Marker's (drawn in code) sit beside the table, and the one whose turn it is is framed in yellow.
    • 
          

        

        

          Eye view (the Top / Eye switch, or V)
          
    
            
  • Low behind the white, looking along the shot, with the cue resting on a bridge hand. Drag left or right to aim, up or down to raise your eye; Power slider and SHOOT.
    • 
            
  • Drawn in 3D with three.js (an open-source WebGL library): the table under a lamp, shiny balls with soft shadows, and a 3D hand. The guide and spin hint are drawn on top through the same camera.
    • 
            
  • three.js is kept inside the game file and started only the first time you open the Eye view, so the game still works with no internet. Without WebGL it falls back to a hand-drawn eye view.

8What Claude Code built, and how it was checked

      
Claude Code wrote the game from the plan. The only borrowed code is the three.js library for the Eye view. The game file has three scripts, plus three.js as plain text:

      

        
sim: the rulesThe table, the ball physics, the referee, the Spin school and Marker. It draws nothing, so test programs can play whole games without a browser.

        
→

        
eye3d: the 3D viewBuilds the table, balls, cue and hand in three.js, and moves them each frame from the game.

        
→

        
game-ui: the screenThe Top view, controls, menus, sound and saving. It asks the rules what happened and shows it.
